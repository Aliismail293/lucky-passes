\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Outcome classes.}
For every question and every RAG configuration we record whether the answer is correct and whether a gold passage
was among the top $k$ retrieved passages (\textit{hit@k}). Crossing the two gives four classes: a \textit{true
pass} (correct, gold retrieved), a \textit{grounding failure} (wrong, gold retrieved), a \textit{lucky pass}
(correct, no gold passage retrieved) and a \textit{miss} (wrong, no gold passage retrieved). Our main measure of
how much an answer-only score overstates retrieval is the lucky-pass rate, which equals answer accuracy minus the
true-pass rate. The main rule counts a question as retrieved when \emph{any} gold passage is in the top $k$; it is
conservative, a lower bound on lucky passes. For multi-hop HotpotQA we also report the \emph{all}-gold version, in
which a question counts as retrieved only if every supporting paragraph is. Both rules were fixed before the full
run.

\paragraph{Memory and guess.}
Each question is also answered \textit{closed-book} (no passages) and with an \textit{oracle} context (the gold
passages only). A lucky pass whose closed-book answer is also correct is \textit{memory lucky}: the model knew the
answer. One whose closed-book answer is wrong is \textit{guess lucky}: the non-gold passages, or chance, produced
it. The oracle condition is the ceiling and flags questions whose gold answer may be wrong.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
\textbf{HotpotQA} \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, distractor dev split: multi-hop questions with gold supporting
paragraphs. The passage pool is every paragraph of every dev question, deduplicated by title, so the sampled
questions compete with the whole dev set's paragraphs rather than only their own ten. Yes/no questions are left out,
since a coin flip would answer half of them and inflate guess-lucky passes.
\textbf{Natural Questions} \citep{kwiatkowski2019nq}: questions and gold passages from BEIR's \texttt{nq}
\citep{thakur2021beir}, joined on normalised question text with the short answers of NQ-open \citep{lee2019latent},
with every gold passage plus a fixed random sample of distractor passages from the BEIR corpus.
\textbf{Invented documents} (control): the bundled demo corpus of ragaudit, documents about a fictional coastal
survey office that no model can have seen in training. Questions are generated from each chunk and checked by a
second model from a different family; the corpus yields fewer questions than the other datasets, and we use all
that pass verification.
\textbf{UTM calendar}: public course-calendar pages of the University of Toronto Mississauga, stored by an
existing student app; real and niche, so only partly memorised. Questions are generated and verified the same way.
Questions are sampled with a fixed seed; the number used per dataset is reported next to every result.

\paragraph{Models.}
Three generators run locally through Ollama at temperature 0 with a fixed seed: \texttt{qwen2.5:7b},
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} and \texttt{llama3.2:3b}. The prompt asks for a short answer and, in the RAG and oracle
conditions, to use the passages; it does not forbid answering from memory, because a typical application prompt
doesn't either.

\paragraph{Retrieval.}
BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25}, dense retrieval with \texttt{nomic-embed-text} (with its query and document
prefixes), and a hybrid that fuses the two rankings with reciprocal rank fusion \citep{cormack2009rrf}. Retrieval
does not depend on the generator, so it is run once per dataset and cached. The core grid crosses all three
retrievers with all three generators at $k = 5$; the $k$-sweep uses the hybrid retriever with \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}
at $k \in \{1, 3, 5\}$.

\paragraph{Judging correctness.}
A normalised contains-match (gold answer string inside the prediction) decides ``correct''. An empty answer or a
non-answer (e.g.\ ``unknown'', ``not specified'') is wrong by rule. Everything else goes to LLM judges from a
different model family than the generator, with a strict prompt and invented worked examples. Answers from
\texttt{qwen2.5:7b} must be accepted by two judges, \texttt{llama3.1:8b} and \texttt{gemma2:9b}; answers from the
\texttt{llama} models are judged by \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}, with a prompt that adds explicit rules for numbers, times and
ranges and for lists of required items. A random share of contains-match decisions is also sent to the LLM judges to
measure agreement between the two signals.
The judge set-up for each generator family was chosen before the full run, by agreement with reference labels on
the pilot's judge-disagreement items (Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the judges' accuracy on a separate, fresh
sample labelled by the author). The reference labels were produced by a stronger model and spot-checked by the
author; they are not human labels.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Every rate is reported with a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval over questions (1,000 resamples, fixed seed) and
its $n$. Comparisons on the same questions (e.g.\ two retrievers, or two values of $k$) use a paired bootstrap of
the difference and an exact McNemar test.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
Written down before the full run.
\textbf{H1}: the lucky-pass rate is high on public datasets and near zero on the invented corpus.
\textbf{H2}: it rises as retrieval weakens (smaller $k$; BM25 on reworded questions).
\textbf{H3}: larger models have more memory-lucky passes.
\textbf{H4}: a dataset's closed-book accuracy predicts its lucky-pass rate.
