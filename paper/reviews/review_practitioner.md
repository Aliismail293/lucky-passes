# Review: "Lucky passes" in retrieval-augmented generation (practitioner reviewer)

Reviewed material: `results/FINDINGS.md`, `paper/FRAMING.md`, `paper/codex/factsheets/results.md`,
`paper/codex/factsheets/limitations.md`, `paper/figures/core/*.png`, `paper/figures/ksweep/lucky_vs_k.png`,
`paper/tables/core/headline_hybrid_k5.tex`, `README.md` (branch `phase-6-paper`). There is no prose yet, so this
review judges the evidence and the story it can support, not the writing.

Reviewer background: I build and evaluate RAG systems in industry. I read this as "should I change how my team scores
our RAG app?"

## Summary

The study defines a *lucky pass* as a RAG answer judged correct when no gold passage is in the top k. It measures
how often lucky passes happen with three small local generators (llama3.2:3b, llama3.1:8b, qwen2.5:7b), three
retrievers (BM25, dense, hybrid) and k in {1, 3, 5}, on four corpora: HotpotQA, NQ, an invented corpus (a control the
model cannot have memorised) and a university course calendar (UTM). Questions for the last two were generated by
the author's open-source tool, ragaudit. Four pre-registered hypotheses are tested with paired bootstrap and McNemar
tests.

The results mostly go against the hypotheses. Under the main rule, lucky passes are 0.0-1.3% on HotpotQA and NQ and 0
on the invented corpus. Hit@5 is 90-99% on the public sets, so there is almost no room for them. H1 and H3 are
not supported, H4 has the wrong sign, and H2 is "mixed". The only dataset with many lucky passes, UTM (12.7-15.4% at
hybrid k = 5, up to ~25% with BM25), turns out, in a post-hoc analysis, to be mostly a measurement artifact.
Boilerplate answers appear in many chunks while only one chunk is labelled gold, and some generated questions don't
name the course they ask about. Removing the boilerplate questions brings qwen/hybrid from 15.3% to 3.9%. A 30-item
error analysis (categorised by an LLM, not a human) finds 1 memory case and 1 guess case. The other 28 are bad
questions, unlabelled gold chunks or judge errors.

The study is honest and carefully instrumented. But as it stands, the takeaway for a practitioner is the reverse of the
premise in the README. Answer-only scores barely overstate retrieval on these setups. Where they seem to, the cause is
usually your eval set's gold labels, not the model's memory. That is a useful message, but the paper as framed in
`paper/FRAMING.md` doesn't lead with it yet.

## Strengths

1. **Honest reporting of negative results.** FINDINGS.md says "not supported" plainly for H1, H3 and H4, marks the UTM
   analysis as post hoc, and keeps the failed pilot gate and the one timed-out call in view rather than tuning them
   away. That is rarer than it should be.
2. **Paired design and sensible statistics.** The same questions go through every condition, with McNemar for the
   paired comparisons, bootstrap CIs everywhere and n on every number. The k-sweep's k = 5 cells match the core run
   exactly, which is a nice consistency check.
3. **The 2x2 framing (true pass / lucky pass / grounding failure / miss)** in README.md is clear, and anyone could put
   it on a dashboard. "Overstatement = accuracy - true-pass rate" is the kind of one-line definition practitioners
   can adopt.
4. **The UTM diagnosis is the most practically useful thing here.** Synthetic eval sets built from one chunk = one
   gold label quietly undercount retrieval when facts repeat across chunks (course fields, product-spec templates,
   policy boilerplate, FAQ duplicates). That happens in every enterprise corpus I've worked on.
5. **The invented-corpus control** is a good idea and a reusable recipe, though see Weakness 4.
6. Cheap, local, cached, restartable pipeline (Ollama + uv, SQLite call cache). Anyone could re-run it.

## Weaknesses

1. **The planned framing doesn't match the evidence, and the one-sentence takeaway is unclear.**
   `paper/FRAMING.md` plans to lead with (1) the memory/guess split and H2, then (2) H3/H4, then the tool. But in
   `results/FINDINGS.md` memory-lucky passes are at most 4.0% anywhere and 0-1.3% on public data. H2 is significant
   only on the artifact dataset, and H3/H4 are not supported. The README still opens with "On a company's private
   documents the model has no memory to fall back on, so the score drops", and the data argue against that claim.
   If I had to say the takeaway in one sentence today, it would be *"With decent retrieval, answer-only RAG accuracy
   is not inflated by memory. Apparent inflation mostly comes from incomplete gold labels in synthetic eval sets."*
   That is a defensible and useful paper, but it is a different paper from the one FRAMING.md plans. The authors
   should pick it on purpose and reorder the contributions (UTM/gold-label finding plus detection recipe first;
   memory split and H2-H4 as supporting negative results).

2. **The headline "practitioner" result is post hoc, rests on one corpus, and is labelled only by an LLM.** The UTM
   finding (FINDINGS.md "The UTM result is mostly a measurement artifact"; results.md section 3) is the strongest
   reason to read the paper. But (a) it was found after the run, (b) it rests on a single 125-chunk calendar, (c) the
   30-item error categories are "Claude's reading, not human labels", and (d) the 50-item human audit
   (`audit/audit_sheet.csv`) is still unlabelled, so every rate rests on LLM judges whose selection agreement (90.3%)
   was measured on the same items, against Claude-Opus labels. For a workshop paper, I would want at least the human
   audit done and the error categories checked by a human before this goes in. Ideally the boilerplate effect would
   also be shown on a second structured corpus (product catalogue, policy manual, API docs) so it isn't one
   calendar's quirk.

3. **The artifact came from the author's own tool, and the paper doesn't yet face this.** UTM and invented questions
   were generated by ragaudit (`lp/datasets.py`, "ragaudit's v2 pipeline"). Its single-source-chunk gold labelling and
   its acceptance of course-less questions ("How is the course delivered?") are what produced the inflated UTM
   rate. Yet FRAMING.md lists ragaudit as "an open tool that flags lucky passes on your own documents" (contribution
   5/3). A practitioner reading that needs to know: (a) does ragaudit now mark every chunk containing the answer as
   gold and reject boilerplate or underspecified questions, (b) if not, would it have reported a 15-25% lucky-pass
   rate on my documents that is mostly false? Right now the evidence says the tool's default question generation
   *creates* false lucky passes. This has to be fixed in the tool, or at least stated as a limitation, before the tool
   is sold as a contribution. A tool section without this would mislead users.

4. **No actionable guidance on when to worry or how to detect it cheaply.** The paper has the ingredients but
   doesn't package them. From these numbers a practitioner could take: "if any-gold hit@k is above ~90%, lucky passes
   are bounded by 100 - hit@k and are negligible" (HotpotQA/NQ rows of `headline_hybrid_k5.tex`), and "if hit@k is
   ~65% but accuracy is ~73% (UTM rows), suspect your gold labels before your model". Please add a short "recipe"
   box: (i) compute the 2x2 per question; (ii) for every lucky pass, string-search the answer in the retrieved
   non-gold chunks (this would have caught 11/30 at no LLM cost); (iii) count how many chunks contain each gold
   answer and flag answers found in N or more chunks (exactly what `scripts/utm_sensitivity.py` does); (iv) run
   closed-book on a sample to separate memory from guessing. Each step is cheap and already in the codebase. Without
   it, the UTM finding reads as an apology for a dataset rather than as a diagnostic method.

5. **The invented-corpus "control" and the UTM closed-book numbers carry signals that aren't discussed.**
   - In `headline_hybrid_k5.tex` the invented corpus has 6-10% closed-book accuracy, and the pilot's pre-registered
     sanity gate (at most 5%) failed (limitations.md). So the control isn't clean. The paper should say what the
     control does and doesn't rule out.
   - UTM closed-book accuracy is 18.0% for qwen2.5:7b vs 1.3-3.4% for the two llamas (table; also visible as the
     isolated green triangle in `paper/figures/core/closed_vs_lucky.png`). qwen2.5:7b also *wrote* these
     questions (limitations.md "Generated questions"). That looks like the self-generation bias the limitations
     section predicts: guessable, boilerplate-style answers fit the generator's own priors. It deserves a sentence
     and, ideally, a check (e.g. qwen's closed-book accuracy on the boilerplate vs non-boilerplate subsets).

6. **The figures show the artifact as the main result, and zeros are invisible.**
   - `paper/figures/core/lucky_by_dataset.png` has bars only for UTM. HotpotQA, NQ and invented show nothing, and a
     reader can't tell "0.0 with CI [0, 0]" from "not run". Put a marker or "0" label on zero bars. More importantly,
     this figure would be the paper's visual headline, and it shows a number the authors themselves call mostly
     artifact. Add the boilerplate-removed UTM rate (e.g. hatched bar or a second marker) next to the raw one, so the
     figure carries the paper's real message.
   - `paper/figures/core/lucky_by_retriever.png` uses llama3.1:8b, while the k-sweep (`lucky_vs_k.png`) and most
     quoted numbers use qwen2.5:7b. Pick one reference model, or explain the switch. The NQ BM25 bar (~0.7%) is
     barely visible.
   - `paper/figures/core/closed_vs_lucky.png` is titled as a question ("Memory predicts lucky passes?"). The nine
     zero-lucky points overlap on the x-axis, and colour (dataset) and shape (model) share one legend without
     subheadings. For H4 with n = 12 points and one cluster driving rho = -0.57, I'd drop the figure, or keep it with
     a caption that says the correlation is driven by UTM.
   - `paper/figures/ksweep/lucky_vs_k.png` is readable and honest. But the UTM decline with k is partly mechanical:
     more chunks means more chance to include the one labelled gold chunk. Combined with the boilerplate
     finding, the "lucky passes rise as retrieval weakens" reading needs that caveat in the caption.

7. **README.md isn't usable for running this on your own RAG app.** It says "No real results yet" (stale). The
   "Reproduce in 3 commands" section reproduces the paper, but there is no path for "point this at my corpus, my
   retriever and my generator". There are no instructions for plugging in an external retriever or pre-computed
   retrieval results, no input format for my own QA pairs with gold chunk IDs, and no mention of the
   boilerplate/multi-gold fix. Given that FRAMING.md sells "a tool to flag lucky passes on your own documents", the
   README should either show that workflow (with a minimal example: input JSONL schema, one command, the 2x2
   output) or clearly point to ragaudit and say what it does differently.

8. **Narrow model and setup range limits the "so what".** The setup is 3B-8B local models, a prompt that permits
   own-knowledge use, and k at most 5. Production systems often use hosted frontier models, where memory-lucky passes
   are plausibly much higher on public-domain facts. The negative H3 result says little about them. Limitations.md
   acknowledges this, but the paper should say plainly that "memory doesn't inflate RAG scores" holds *for small
   models with high-recall retrieval*. One hosted-model spot check (even 150 NQ questions) would make the result
   far more relevant to practitioners.

## Questions for the authors

1. Can you state the paper's main contribution in one sentence now that H1, H3 and H4 are not supported? Is it the
   gold-label/boilerplate finding, the tool, or the negative replication of Jonah et al.?
2. Has ragaudit been changed to mark every answer-containing chunk as gold and to reject boilerplate or
   underspecified questions? If you re-generate the UTM questions with those fixes, what is the lucky-pass rate?
   That would turn the post-hoc analysis into a confirmatory one.
3. What is the human-audit (50 items) judge accuracy, split into lucky passes and the rest? Especially: how often does
   the LLM judge accept incomplete list answers (6/30 in the LLM-labelled sample)?
4. Does qwen2.5:7b's 18% closed-book accuracy on UTM (vs 1-3% for the llamas) come from the boilerplate questions it
   generated itself?
5. The secondary (all-gold) rule gives 6.7-24.7% on HotpotQA: correct answers with only half the evidence. Isn't that
   the more practically relevant number for multi-hop enterprise questions? Why does it stay in the background?
6. Would a cheap answer-string match over all retrieved chunks ("answer present in context") replace the gold-label
   check entirely for practitioners who don't have gold labels? How well would it agree with your classification?
7. How many lucky passes would remain if the RAG prompt said "answer only from the passages"?

## Minor issues

- README.md: "No real results yet" is stale; "the score drops" on private documents contradicts the findings.
- The fact sheet points to `paper/tables/core/main_table.tex` as the headline table, while the new hybrid-k5 table is
  `headline_hybrid_k5.tex`. They hold the same rows (only the header comment differs). Pick one for the paper.
- `headline_hybrid_k5.tex`: dataset names are lower-case identifiers (`hotpotqa`, `nq`, `utm`). Use display names
  (HotpotQA, NQ, UTM calendar). The table also has no UTM-without-boilerplate row, which I would add.
- In the table, CIs of [0.0, 0.0] on n = 48 and n = 150 suggest more certainty than the data give. A percentile
  bootstrap can't produce a non-zero interval from 0 successes. Use Wilson or Clopper-Pearson intervals for
  zero counts (e.g. 0/150 gives an upper 95% bound of about 2.4%; 0/48 gives about 7.4%).
- Figure titles hard-code the setup ("hybrid, k=5"); move it to the caption, and use consistent model order
  (by size) across figures and table.
- "Lucky" mixes memory, guess, unlabelled gold and judge error. Consider renaming the unlabelled-gold case
  ("ungrounded-by-label") so that "lucky" means what a reader expects.
- FINDINGS.md reports judge agreement 77.8% between contains-match and LLM. Say which direction matters for the
  lucky-pass rate (the LLM is the lenient one, which inflates it).

## Score and confidence

**Score: 2 / 5** (below borderline, as it stands). The measurement work is careful and honest, and there is a
genuinely useful practitioner finding inside it: synthetic eval sets with single-chunk gold labels make grounded
answers look lucky. But the planned framing leads with hypotheses the data didn't support. The useful finding is
post hoc, rests on one corpus and LLM-only labels, and was produced by the very tool the paper offers as a
contribution. With the human audit done, a confirmatory re-run of UTM using fixed question generation, a short
detection recipe, and the figures and README reworked around that message, I would expect this to be a clear 3.5-4
at an applied workshop.

**Confidence: 4 / 5.** I know RAG evaluation in practice well. I didn't re-run anything or check the code beyond the
dataset loader, and judged the paper from fact sheets rather than prose.
